Contested Ground: Solo Mode

Playing an Open Strategy Game Alone

Author

Roberto Bisceglie

This document is a complete rules set for playing an Open Strategy Game alone. It builds on Contested Ground, but everything you need at the table is here. The Open Strategy Game Handbook explains the reasoning behind the core rules; Factionlog is a notation for keeping your game record.

Part I: Overview

What Solo Play Is

You play one faction. Every other faction is an Autonomous Actor (AA): a faction driven by a short brief, a behavior rule and a table of actions. You are also the Referee: you adjudicate every action, yours included, and write the Report.

A group OSG runs on four things a single player does not have: rivals who make decisions, a social layer where deals are struck and broken, information you do not hold, and a Referee who answers questions you could not answer yourself. Solo Mode replaces each one.

A group game has… Solo Mode uses…
Other players Autonomous Actors (Part IV)
Negotiation between turns Disposition and Offers (Part V)
Hidden briefs and covert play Secrets (Part VI)
Pressure from rival players The Crisis Clock (Part VII)
A Referee who answers questions The Oracle (Part III)
A Referee who improvises Inspiration Tables (Part VIII)

Everything else is unchanged: the Problem, the Brief, one action per turn, the Action / Outcome / Leverage format, the dice, the RAT check, No Action Without Friction, the Report and the Debrief.

Two Rules of Honesty

Solo play works only if you do not cheat yourself. Two rules carry the weight:

  1. Commit before you know. You write your action before any AA acts. You never change it after.
  2. When you do not know, ask. If the fiction does not settle a question, do not decide it in your favor. Ask the Oracle.

What You Need

  • At least two six-sided dice
  • A scenario: a Problem, your Brief, and 2–4 AAs. Appendix A is ready to play; Appendix C generates new ones; Part IX tells you what a scenario needs.
  • A record of play: a notebook or a text file. Factionlog notation is recommended.
  • The Turn Sheet (Part XII)

Part II: The Core Rules in Brief

These are the rules of Contested Ground, condensed. They apply to you and to every AA alike.

The action. Each faction takes exactly one action per turn, written as:

Action: What specifically is the faction doing?
Outcome: What result does it want?
Leverage: Why should this work? Ground it in established fiction: resources, relationships, position, prior actions.

Unopposed actions. If nothing opposes the action and the fiction raises no obstacle, the desired outcome occurs. No roll. Decide how it lands and what mark it leaves.

Opposed actions. Grade leverage Strong (the faction has the advantage) or Weak (plausible, but no strong basis). Roll 2d6.

Leverage Keep
Strong The higher die
Weak The lower die
Result Outcome
6 Desired outcome occurs, and something especially good may also occur
4–5 Desired outcome occurs
2–3 The action proceeds, but the outcome is worse than desired
1 Outcome worse than desired, and something especially bad may also occur

Strong produces the desired outcome 75% of the time; Weak, 25%.

Actions in conflict. When two actions oppose each other directly, grade and roll each. If both outcomes can be true, both occur as rolled. If not, the higher kept die prevails and the other outcome is worse than desired. A tie is a standoff: both worse than desired.

Joint actions. Factions may pool their turns into one action. Each participant spends its action and names its specific contribution. One grade on the combined leverage, one roll; the outcome lands on all of them.

Bonuses. A named, spendable asset. Declare it in the Leverage section. It raises Weak to Strong; on an action already Strong, it turns a kept 4–5 into a 6.

Force of Nature. When both dice show the same number on an adjudication roll, you may introduce an event from outside any faction’s control (Part VIII). Once or twice per game.

RAT. Before writing any outcome, check: is it Reasonable (proportionate), Actionable (it leaves a hook for someone), Traceable (earlier fiction led here)?

No Action Without Friction. Every outcome leaves a mark. A success with no complicating consequence is not finished: ask who notices, what it costs, what it opens up. Complicate; do not block.

Part III: The Oracle

The Oracle answers any yes/no question about the world that the fiction has not settled: whether a mercenary company is for hire, whether a road is open, whether an AA accepts your offer.

Asking

  1. Phrase a yes/no question.
  2. Set the odds from the established fiction:
Odds Roll Yes (4+)
Likely 2d6, keep the higher 75%
Even 1d6 50%
Unlikely 2d6, keep the lower 25%
  1. Read the result:
Die Answer
6 Yes, and…
5 Yes
4 Yes, but…
3 No, but…
2 No
1 No, and…

“And” strengthens the answer; “but” softens it. Interpret it in the way that best fits the fiction, then write the answer down. It is now established fiction.

Oracle Discipline

  • Choose odds before you roll. If you cannot decide between two levels, take the one closer to Even.
  • Ask once. Do not ask the same question again unless the fiction has changed.
  • Ask what you would ask a Referee. The Oracle replaces a Referee’s ruling, not your decisions. Never ask it what your own faction should do.
  • Doubles mean nothing on an Oracle roll. Force of Nature applies only to adjudication.

Open Questions

For questions that are not yes/no, use these:

  • Which faction? Number the factions involved and roll.
  • Which thread? Keep a numbered list of open threads (unresolved hooks from past Reports). Roll for one.
  • What kind of thing? Use the Inspiration Tables (Part VIII) and interpret.

Grading Leverage

Grading is the place where solo play goes wrong most easily: you will be tempted to call your own leverage Strong and your rivals’ Weak. Before calling any faction’s leverage Strong, check:

All three yes: Strong. Any clear no: Weak. If you genuinely cannot tell, ask the Oracle, “Is the leverage Strong?”, at Even odds. Apply the same checklist to every AA, above all to an AA acting against you.

Part IV: Autonomous Actors

The AA Brief

Each AA has:

Field Content
Objective One sentence: what it wants most
Position One or two specific assets or vulnerabilities that matter
Leverage Type Military, Economic, Geographic, Informational, Legitimacy, Popular, or the scenario’s own
Behavior Rule How it chooses what to do (below)
Disposition Its attitude toward you (Part V)
Action Table Six actions it might take (below)
Secret Table Six things that might be true about it (Part VI)
Bonus (optional) One named bonus

Behavior Rules

Roll d6 for each AA at setup, or choose.

d6 Behavior Rule
1 Consolidator Secures what it holds. Acts against another faction only if that faction threatened it in the last Report.
2 Aggressor Pushes toward its objective by the most direct means. Never holds two turns running.
3 Opportunist Targets the faction lowest on the Standing list.
4 Balancer Targets the faction highest on the Standing list.
5 Reactor Holds until acted upon. Then targets the faction that last affected it.
6 Schemer Acts on odd turns, holds on even turns. Keeps deals only while they serve it.

Holding. When its rule says an AA holds (a Reactor not acted upon last turn, a Schemer on an even turn, a Consolidator with nothing to defend), skip its table. It consolidates: an unopposed action that secures something it already has. Write it in the Report in one line.

An AA never targets itself. If its rule points at itself, take the next faction down (Opportunist) or up (Balancer) the list.

Standing

Behavior rules need to know who looks strong and who looks exposed. Keep a Standing list of every faction, yours included, from Dominant at the top to Exposed at the bottom.

  • Set it at setup from the Briefs.
  • Revise it at the end of every turn, from the Report: who gained visible power, who lost it, who overreached.
  • Judge by what a rival could see, not by what you privately know. A faction with a hidden weakness still looks strong until the weakness is public.
  • If two factions are too close to order, roll.

The Action Table

Write a d6 table of six actions for each AA, consistent with its objective, leverage type and behavior rule. A balanced table:

d6 Kind of action
1–2 Direct moves toward its objective
3–4 Moves against a [target]: pressure, obstruction, attack, investigation
5 A positional move: secure an asset, build a relationship, prepare
6 Escalation: the boldest thing it would plausibly do

Each entry is a verb and an object, short enough to read at a glance: “Blockade the coast road against [target].”

Filling [target]. If the behavior rule names a target (Opportunist, Balancer, Reactor), use it. Otherwise the target is the faction most in the way of the AA’s objective. Between equal candidates, an AA that is Hostile or Wary toward you picks you; one that is Open or Allied does not.

Writing the AA’s action. A table entry is a direction, not a finished action. Expand it into full Action / Outcome / Leverage, written as a competent player of that faction would write it, using its best established leverage. Play every AA to win. An AA played to lose is a game you have already won, and not worth playing.

Veto. If the rolled entry makes no sense in the current fiction (the target is gone, the location destroyed) or breaks the AA’s behavior rule (a Consolidator rolling an attack when no one threatened it), reroll once. If the reroll fails too, write an action from the behavior rule directly.

Bonuses. An AA with a bonus spends it the first time it acts within the bonus’s scope with Weak leverage, or in an action in conflict.

Adaptation

AAs change as the game does. Rewrite one entry of an AA’s Action Table, replacing the entry least relevant to the current fiction, when:

  • the AA’s kept die is a 6 or a 1 on an adjudication roll;
  • its objective is achieved: it takes a new objective, the next step its leverage makes possible;
  • its objective becomes impossible: it takes a new objective consistent with its identity, and you roll a new behavior rule.

Elimination and New Powers

A faction can be eliminated: destroyed, absorbed, or reduced to irrelevance. When an AA is eliminated, ask the Oracle at the start of the next turn: “Does a new power rise to fill the gap?” (Likely if what it held still matters.) On a yes, create a minimal AA: objective, position, leverage type, behavior rule, a six-entry table. Its disposition toward you starts at Wary.

Part V: Disposition and Offers

Talking is free. In Solo Mode, talking happens through Offers, and the AAs’ memory of how you have treated them is their Disposition.

Disposition

Each AA has a disposition toward you on a five-step scale:

Hostile → Wary → Neutral → Open → Allied

Set the starting disposition from the Briefs: Wary if its objectives conflict with yours, Open if they align, Neutral otherwise. The scenario may set it directly.

Disposition Offers Targeting Keeping deals
Hostile Refuses unless the deal serves its objective; then Unlikely Picks you whenever its rule allows a choice n/a
Wary One step worse Picks you between equal candidates Even
Neutral No change No preference Likely
Open One step better Does not pick you between equal candidates Likely
Allied One step better; accepts without rolling if the deal serves its objective Never targets you unless its rule forces it Kept without a roll (Schemer: Likely)

Moving disposition. At the end of each turn, from the Report:

  • +1 if you kept a deal with it, or your action clearly benefited it
  • −1 if your action harmed it, even as a side effect, or it uncovered a covert action of yours aimed at it
  • −2 if you broke a deal with it

Apply each cause once per turn. Disposition never moves past Hostile or Allied.

Making an Offer

At the start of each turn you may make one Offer to each AA.

  1. State the deal. What you give and what you ask. Be specific: “Essaveth will allow Meranto’s agents through the passes; Meranto will delay Caldrath’s next loan.”
  2. Set the odds. Start at Even, then shift one step for each that applies:
    • Better: the deal clearly advances its objective; its disposition is Open or Allied
    • Worse: the deal costs it something its behavior rule protects, or helps its rival; its disposition is Wary
    The odds run Refused → Unlikely → Even → Likely → Accepted. Past Likely, it accepts without rolling; past Unlikely, it refuses.
  3. Ask the Oracle: “Does [AA] accept?”
Answer Result
Yes, and… Accepts, and adds something: information, extra help, or +1 disposition
Yes Accepts
Yes, but… Accepts on one added condition. Take it or walk away.
No, but… Refuses, but says what it would accept instead
No Refuses
No, and… Refuses and takes offense (−1 disposition), or leaks the offer to a rival (a crumb in the Report)

Deals

Keep a Deals list: who, what, since which turn, until when.

  • Obligations replace the table. If a deal requires the AA to act this turn, that is its action; do not roll its table. If a deal requires it to refrain (not attack you, not close a route), table results that break the deal are rerolled.
  • Deals can break. When keeping a deal would cost the AA something real this turn, or every turn for a Schemer, ask: “Does [AA] keep the deal?” with odds from its disposition (table above). On a no, it breaks the deal, and its action this turn exploits the break. Strike the deal; the betrayal goes in the Report.
  • Your side counts too. If you break a deal, its disposition drops by 2 and the break goes in the Report.

Joint Actions with an AA

An Offer can propose a joint action. If accepted, each turn the joint action runs ask: “Does [AA] confirm?” (Likely; Allied confirms without rolling). On a no, the joint action proceeds without its contribution, the leverage is regraded, and the AA rolls its own table as normal.

Signals

AAs talk to you too. After adjudication, roll d6 for each AA:

d6 Signal
1–2 Silence
3 Warning. It warns you off something it cares about. Write the warning as a crumb in the Report.
4 Offer. It proposes a deal, drawn from its objective. Answer at your next Offer step. Refusing an Open or Allied AA costs −1 disposition.
5 Exposure. It has overextended. Name the vulnerability in the Report.
6 Windfall. Its action this turn incidentally helps you. Write it as an unintended consequence.

Part VI: Secrets

You wrote every brief, so in principle you know everything. Secrets fix this by keeping the truth undecided until play uncovers it.

Secret Tables

Each AA has a Secret Table: six things that might be true about it. Only the entry rolled when the secret is uncovered becomes true; the other five never were. You know the possibilities, not the answer.

Write a table for each AA at setup, or use the generic table for its leverage type below (Popular is in Appendix C).

d6 Military Economic Geographic
1 Its troops are unpaid; their loyalty is for sale It is insolvent; its wealth is borrowed There is a second way around its position, and it knows
2 A senior commander answers to someone else It is secretly financing a rival Its hold depends on a local who can be turned
3 Its supplies will run out sooner than anyone knows One creditor or client could ruin it The position is decaying: flood, disease, collapse
4 It has hidden a recent defeat Its records conceal a crime It has promised passage to someone
5 It is preparing to strike an unexpected target It has already sold something it publicly holds Its territory hides a resource no one has counted
6 Half its strength exists only on paper It is quietly buying control of something central It plans to close the route at the worst moment for someone
d6 Informational Legitimacy
1 It has a spy in your camp Its authority rests on a contested or forged document
2 Its best source is feeding it lies Its leadership is divided and close to splitting
3 It holds a document that would ruin a third faction It has promised recognition to two rivals
4 It has been leaking to a rival A scandal is waiting to break
5 It knows one of your private Position facts Its own founding precedent supports a rival’s claim
6 Its reputation rests on a lie It will recognize whoever pays enough

Uncovering Secrets

Roll on an AA’s Secret Table when:

  • You investigate it and succeed. On a 4–5, the secret is yours, privately. On a 6, roll twice and keep both, or keep one and gain a crumb on another faction. On a 2–3, it is a rumor: roll it, but it is unconfirmed until a second investigation or until it pays off in play.
  • It rolls a 1 on any adjudication. The secret comes out publicly in the Report.
  • A Signal reveals Exposure and the vulnerability fits a secret better than a new invention.

An uncovered secret is established fiction: it counts toward Strong leverage. Cross it off; reroll duplicates.

Your Own Secrets

Your private Brief and your covert actions are secrets too.

  • Covert actions. Write the outcome privately and plant a crumb in the Report as usual. At the end of the turn, ask: “Is it noticed?” Unlikely; Even if any AA has Informational leverage or the action targeted an AA. On a yes, roll to see which interested AA notices: its disposition drops by 1, it now knows, and its next [target] is you.
  • Investigated by an AA. When an AA’s action investigates you and succeeds, it learns one of your private Position facts or a past covert action of your choice. The AA may use it as leverage from then on.

Part VII: The Crisis Clock

Rival players keep a group game under pressure. In Solo Mode, the Problem itself does.

The Clock

The Crisis Clock is a track of boxes. Set its length to the number of turns minus one, unless the scenario fixes it: left alone, the crisis breaks before the last turn, and the final turn is played in its aftermath.

Mark three Escalations on the track, roughly a third, two thirds, and the last box. The last is the Breaking Point.

  • Advance one box at the end of every turn.
  • Advance one more when an outcome’s “something especially bad” or a Force of Nature makes the Problem worse.
  • Push it back one box when an action whose Outcome addresses the Problem itself, not a rival, rolls a 6.

When the clock reaches an Escalation, it happens in that turn’s Report, as a fact every faction must respond to.

Writing Escalations

Each Escalation is a specific worsening of the Problem. A good Escalation:

  • follows from the Problem statement, visibly (Traceable);
  • hurts at least two factions, and in different ways;
  • opens at least one option that did not exist before.

The Breaking Point is what happens if no one resolves the crisis: the default bad ending the Problem implies. It does not end the game. The remaining turn is played in the new world it creates, and that is often the most interesting turn of the game.

Example: the Crisis Clock of the Corentine Succession is in Appendix A. Its Breaking Point is the lapse of the bilateral treaties.

Part VIII: Inspiration Tables

Use these when a result calls for something you have not thought of. Roll, then interpret through the fiction. If the result does not fit, take the nearest entry that does.

Something Especially Good (on a 6)

d6 The faction also…
1 Gains an unexpected ally: a third party offers support
2 Learns something: roll a Secret of a faction involved
3 Establishes a fact that will support its next action in the same area
4 Weakens a rival: move the opposing faction down one place in Standing
5 Wins goodwill: one AA involved moves +1 disposition toward the actor
6 Eases the crisis locally: push the Crisis Clock back one box

Something Especially Bad (on a 1)

d6 The faction also…
1 Is exposed: a covert action or a private fact becomes public
2 Provokes backlash: one AA involved moves −1 disposition toward the actor
3 Loses an asset: a bonus, a holding, or a relationship
4 Strengthens a rival: move the opposing faction up one place in Standing
5 Overreaches: the attempt creates a new vulnerability; add it to the Position
6 Worsens the crisis: advance the Crisis Clock one box

When an AA is the actor, read entries 2 and 5 as disposition toward the faction it acted on, if that faction is you.

Friction

Use when an outcome leaves no mark.

d6 The outcome…
1 Was witnessed: name who saw it
2 Cost more than planned
3 Alarmed someone: the faction most affected now has a reason to act
4 Set a precedent others can invoke
5 Opened a door for someone else
6 Is incomplete: one piece remains, to be finished later

Force of Nature

Use on doubles, when you choose to. Roll the source, then tie it to the most recent unresolved thread.

d6 Source
1 Weather or terrain: a storm, a flood, a collapsed road
2 Disease, fire or disaster
3 Popular unrest: a crowd, a strike, a riot
4 An outside power takes an interest
5 An economic shock: a failed harvest, a collapsed price
6 The past returns: a dead person’s letter, an old claim, a forgotten treaty

Part IX: Setting Up

To generate the whole scenario with dice instead, use Appendix C.

  1. The Problem. One sentence that names what has changed, implies competing interests, and points at no correct answer.
  2. Your Actor. A full Brief: a short-term and a long-term objective, three Position bullets, at least one named bonus.
  3. The AAs. Two to four, for three to five factions in total. Fewer than the 5–8 of a group game: you run every AA by hand. Give each an AA Brief, an Action Table and a Secret Table. Give each a different leverage type from yours and from each other’s where you can.
  4. The Map. Three to five named locations, at least one contested.
  5. The Crisis Clock. Length, three Escalations, the Breaking Point.
  6. Standing. Order every faction from Dominant to Exposed.
  7. Disposition. Set each AA’s starting disposition toward you.
  8. Turn Zero. Ask the Oracle up to five questions about the world that matter for your first move, and record the answers. Then write down one or two things you deliberately leave unsettled.

A game of three factions (you and two AAs) over six turns is a solid first run. The lighter game in Appendix A is exactly that.

Turns Feel
4 Sharp and fast: one strategic arc
6 Standard: alliances form and break
8 Full campaign: long-term objectives come into reach

The game ends at the stated turn count. No extensions.

Part X: The Turn

1. Take Stock

Read the last Report. Check the Standing list, the Deals list, and the Crisis Clock.

2. Talk

Answer any Offer an AA made last turn. Make up to one Offer to each AA (Part V).

3. Declare Your Action

Write your action in full: Action / Outcome / Leverage. Declare any bonus you spend. From here on, it does not change.

4. Generate AA Actions

For each AA, in order:

  1. If a deal requires an action, that is its action. Check whether it keeps the deal if keeping it costs something.
  2. If its behavior rule says it holds, it consolidates.
  3. Otherwise, roll its Action Table, fill any [target], and expand the entry into a full Action / Outcome / Leverage.

5. Adjudicate

  1. Identify which actions are unopposed, opposed, in conflict, or joint.
  2. Grade leverage for every opposed action (Part III checklist; Oracle if unsure).
  3. Roll. Apply 6s and 1s with the Inspiration Tables. Consider a Force of Nature on doubles.
  4. Check every outcome against RAT and No Action Without Friction.
  5. Uncover Secrets where results call for it (Part VI).

6. Signals and Notice

Roll a Signal for each AA. For each covert action you took, ask whether it is noticed.

7. Update

  • Disposition: apply the turn’s changes.
  • Deals: add, strike, note breaks.
  • Adaptation: rewrite table entries where a 6, a 1, or an objective change calls for it.
  • Crisis Clock: advance it; if it hits an Escalation, it lands now.
  • Standing: reorder from what the Report shows.

8. Write the Report

Third person, news-roundup style, short.

  1. Turn header: one sentence on where things stand.
  2. One paragraph per action: the outcome as fact, its friction, at least one hook.
  3. Crumbs for every private outcome, yours and the AAs’.
  4. The Clock: one line if an Escalation landed.
  5. Closing observation: one open thread no single faction owns.

Add new hooks to your thread list. After each paragraph, ask: does this put something in front of me or an AA that changes the next turn? If not, rewrite it.

Part XI: The Debrief

After the final Report, write a closing frame: one line per faction on where it ends. Then:

  1. Your objectives. Did you achieve the short-term one? Did you achieve or lay groundwork for the long-term one? Why?
  2. The AAs’ objectives. For each AA, did it get what it wanted? Answer from the fiction; if the fiction does not settle it, ask the Oracle at Even. Compare your result with theirs. That comparison is the closest Solo Mode comes to a winner.
  3. The surprise. Which turn surprised you most, and what produced it: an AA, the Clock, a Secret, or your own action’s consequences?
  4. The design. What would you change: the Problem, an AA’s behavior or table, the Clock?

A solo game that produced no surprises had AA tables that were too predictable or a Clock that was too slow. A game that felt incoherent had a Problem that did not generate distinct competing interests.

Appendix A: The Corentine Succession (Solo)

A ready-to-play scenario for Solo Mode. It is the worked example scenario from the Open Strategy Game Handbook, Appendix G, with everything Solo Mode needs. You can play any of the five factions; the other four are AAs.

Full game: you and four AAs, six turns. Expect a long first turn.
Lighter game: Essaveth, Caldrath and House Meranto only, six turns. Saivorn and the Conclave remain in the world as background institutions: they exist and can be named in actions, but take no actions and have no disposition. This is the recommended first game, and the one used in Appendix B.

The Problem

The death of Arbiter Vethara has left the Corentine Peninsula without a recognized authority to renew the bilateral treaties that have kept its five polities at peace for thirty years.

The World

Five polities have coexisted under bilateral treaties ratified by the Arbiter, a title held for thirty years by Vethara of Corenth. The Arbiter held no army and claimed no territory; her authority rested on personal standing, the Conclave’s ratification, and everyone’s interest in having someone to call when things went wrong. She died twelve days ago and named no successor. The treaties have no renewal mechanism that all parties recognize.

The Map

Location Notes
Corenth The central city, seat of the old Arbiter, site of the Conclave’s extraterritorial temple. Contested.
The Northern Passes Essaveth’s territory between Caldrath and Corenth. Caldrath’s army is camped at their foot; Corenth is three days’ march beyond them.
Caldrath The kingdom’s heartland, to the north.
Saivorn The coastal republic to the south, with the peninsula’s only two deep-water ports. Reaches Corenth by sea.
The Southern Coast Merchant country with no single power; House Meranto’s offices are here, but its leverage is everywhere.

Turn Zero: Established

These answers are settled before play. Do not ask the Oracle about them.

  • The treaties remain in force until they lapse unrenewed at the end of Turn 4 (the Crisis Clock’s Breaking Point). Any faction may try to hasten or delay the lapse through action.
  • Caldrath’s historical claim on Essaveth’s northern province is dormant until Caldrath formally declares it.

Appendix C: Scenario Generator

Build a new solo scenario in about half an hour. The tables are genre-neutral: read every entry through the setting you have in mind, whether that is fantasy kingdoms, Renaissance city-states, a colonial frontier, a mining colony or a boardroom.

Reading d66: roll two dice, one after the other. The first is the tens, the second the units: a 3 then a 5 is 35.

When a result does not fit, reroll it or bend it. When two results seem to contradict each other, look twice before rerolling: the gap between them is often where the scenario is.

Procedure

  1. The Problem: roll an Event, an Absence and a Stake; write one sentence.
  2. Factions: for each of three to five factions, roll an Archetype, a secondary leverage, a Stance, a Vulnerability and a Tie.
  3. Briefs: turn each faction’s rolls into objectives and a Position. One becomes yours; the rest become AAs.
  4. AA tables: give each AA the generic Action Table and Secret Table for its leverage type, then customize at least two entries of each.
  5. The Crisis Clock: two Escalations and a Breaking Point.
  6. The Map: the seat of the absence, plus a location for each faction and one or two more.
  7. Check it.

3. Briefs

  • Position: three bullets. One asset from the Archetype and its leverage, one Vulnerability, one Tie. Ask of each: could two people read this bullet and argue different things from it? If so, make it more specific.
  • Bonus: one named, scoped bonus for your faction; optionally one per AA.
  • Your faction: pick one, or roll. Everyone else becomes an AA: objective (the short-term one), position (the asset and the vulnerability), leverage, behavior rule (the Stance’s suggestion, or roll), starting disposition (Wary if its objectives conflict with yours, Open if they align, Neutral otherwise).

4 Defection: someone important inside one faction changes sides
5 Public anger: crowds gather and demand that someone act
6 Deadline: a date is set after which something becomes final

Check each Escalation: does it hurt at least two factions, differently? Does it open an option that did not exist before?

6. The Map

Start with the seat of the absence: the place where what is missing used to live (the capital, the court, the dam, the temple). It is contested. Give each faction a home location. Then roll one or two more locations, so the map has three to five places in all.

d6 Location
1 A crossing: a pass, a bridge, a ford
2 A harbour or station
3 A source: a mine, a well, rich fields
4 A stronghold
5 A market or exchange
6 A shrine, archive or court

Draw the connections. Ask which faction must pass through another’s ground to reach the contested place. That route is where the first conflict will happen.

7. Check It

Example

Rolls: Event 53 (a flood moves the border river), Absence 23 (a treaty that holds the borders), Stake 1 (open war).

The spring flood that moved the Varn into its old bed has left the Three Valleys without a treaty that holds their borders.

Three factions:

Archetype Leverage Stance Vulnerability Tie
33 River toll lord, the House of Esk Geographic / Economic Threatened 5 Its toll stations depend on the river’s course 2 Is owed by the garrison
13 Border garrison of Harrow Ford Military / Legitimacy Claimant 3 Its supplies last weeks, not months 1 Owes the toll lord
52 The Valley Court Legitimacy / Informational Guardian 6 Has no force of its own 4 Secret understanding with the garrison
  • The House of Esk (Threatened). Short-term: “Get the Court and the garrison to swear our stations stand, before the first blood is spilled!” Long-term: “Write the toll rights into whatever treaty redraws the Varn!” Its toll stations are now on the wrong bank.
  • Harrow Ford (Claimant). Short-term: “Plant the garrison’s banner on the new bank first!” Long-term: “Make the garrison commander the warden of the Three Valleys!” It owes Esk for a winter’s grain, and its stores will not last the summer.
  • The Valley Court (Guardian). Short-term: “No new border is lawful until the Court rules on it!” Long-term: “Put the Court’s survey above every claim in the valleys!” It has no force of its own, and it has quietly promised the garrison commander a favorable ruling.

The conflicts are already there. The garrison needs Esk’s grain and wants Esk’s ground. The Court’s secret promise to the garrison betrays the river lord who believes the Court will protect him. Escalations, rolled: 1 Blood (a toll keeper drowned at the new ford) and 3 Outside interest (the duke downstream sends surveyors). The Breaking Point is open war between the valleys.

Glossary

AA (Autonomous Actor): A faction run by its brief, behavior rule and tables instead of a player.

Action Table: Six actions an AA might take, rolled on d6 each turn it acts.

Behavior Rule: How an AA chooses what to do and whom to target: Consolidator, Aggressor, Opportunist, Balancer, Reactor, Schemer.

Crisis Clock: A track that advances each turn as the Problem worsens, with three Escalations ending in a Breaking Point.

Deal: An agreement with an AA, struck through an Offer. Its obligations replace the AA’s table roll; it may break.

Disposition: An AA’s attitude toward you: Hostile, Wary, Neutral, Open, Allied. Sets offer odds, targeting and deal-keeping.

Escalation: A specific worsening of the Problem, triggered when the Crisis Clock reaches its box.

Holding: An AA’s turn of consolidation when its behavior rule says it does not act.

Offer: A deal you propose to an AA, answered by the Oracle.

Oracle: A yes/no question answered with dice: Likely keeps the higher of 2d6, Even rolls 1d6, Unlikely keeps the lower.

Rumor: A Secret rolled on a partial investigation result; unconfirmed until verified or until it pays off.

Secret Table: Six things that might be true about an AA. Only the one rolled when uncovered becomes true.

Signal: An AA’s unprompted communication, rolled after adjudication: warning, offer, exposure or windfall.

Stance: In the Scenario Generator, how the Problem touches a faction: Claimant, Guardian, Threatened, Opportunist, Creditor or Outsider. It sets the faction’s objectives.

Standing: A ranking of every faction from Dominant to Exposed, revised each turn, used by AA behavior rules.

Thread: An unresolved hook from a past Report, kept on a numbered list.
